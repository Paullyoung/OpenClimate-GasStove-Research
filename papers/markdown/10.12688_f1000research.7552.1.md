## Abstract  
Household air pollution is a leading cause of disability-adjusted life years in Southeast Asia and the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years globally. There are at least sixty sources of household air pollution, and these vary from country to country. Indoor tobacco smoking, construction material used in building houses, fuel used for cooking, heating and lighting, use of incense and various forms of mosquito repellents, use of pesticides and chemicals used for cleaning at home, and use of artificial fragrances are some of the various sources that contribute to household air pollution.

Household air pollution affects all stages of life with multi-systemic health effects, and its effects are evident right from pre-conception to old age. In utero exposure to household air pollutants has been shown to have health effects that resonate over the entire lifetime. Exposures to indoor air pollutants in early childhood also tend to have repercussions throughout life. The respiratory system bears the maximum brunt, but effects on the cardiovascular system, endocrine system, and nervous system are largely underplayed. Household air pollutants have also been implicated in the development of various types of cancers.  

Identifying household air pollutants and their health implications helps us prepare for various health-related issues. However, the real challenge is adopting changes to reduce the health effects of household air pollution and designing innovative interventions to minimize the risk of further exposure.  

This review is an attempt to understand the various sources of household air pollution, the effects on health, and strategies to deal with this emergent risk factor of global mortality and morbidity.  

**Keywords:** air quality, particulate matter, air pollutants

## Introduction  
In the words of Rosalynn Carter, "There is nothing more important than a good, safe and secure home." It is believed that a house is the most secure and healthy environment for any individual. However, the house can also be a source of various air pollutants that can have a significant adverse impact on health.  

According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) report, 8 million people die every year globally because of air pollution. Among these, 4.3 million die because of air pollution from household sources, and 3.7 million die because of ambient air pollution (Figure 1).  

According to the Global Burden of Disease Report, household air pollution is the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in Southeast Asia and the third leading cause of DALYs globally. Interestingly, ambient air pollution is the sixth leading cause of DALYs in the Southeast Asia region and the ninth leading cause of DALYs globally. Until recently, air pollution from motor vehicles and industries was perceived as a bigger threat to human health. It is only now that we have started appreciating the significant adverse impacts of household air pollution on human health.

## Household Conditions and Pollution Sources  
Modern households are often tightly constructed to ensure energy efficiency and thermal comfort but can become reservoirs for various indoor pollutants. Certain lifestyle and construction-related features prevalent in households contribute to indoor air pollution.  

- Houses are often built with materials containing fire-retardant chemicals to reduce fire hazards.  
- Flooring materials include polished or varnished wood, vinyl, or carpeted surfaces, all of which may emit volatile organic compounds (VOCs).  
- Commonly used heating sources include electric furnaces or wood fireplaces, particularly in colder regions.  
- Insulation materials, while thermal-efficient, can also trap airborne pollutants.  
- Upholstery such as heavy drapes, thick sofa coverings, decorative throws, and pillows are likely to collect dust and allergens.  
- Furnishings may be constructed from varnished wood or particle boards, which can emit formaldehyde and other toxic substances.  

In addition to these, the use of mosquito repellents, incense sticks, tobacco products, cleaning chemicals, and artificial fragrances further worsens indoor air quality, increasing exposure to particulate matter and toxic gases.

## Notable Health Impacts  
Household air pollution is linked to adverse health effects, spanning all age groups and organ systems. Below are major findings from the reviewed literature:  

1. **In Utero Effects:** Exposure to household air pollutants during pregnancy can adversely affect the fetus and have health consequences that persist throughout the individual’s life.  
2. **Childhood:** Young children exposed to indoor pollutants may suffer from both chronic respiratory diseases and long-term developmental impacts on the nervous system.  
3. **Respiratory System:** The respiratory system is most affected by household air pollution. Conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and pneumonia are commonly linked to prolonged exposure.  
4. **Cardiovascular System:** Indoor air pollutants also contribute to cardiovascular diseases like hypertension and ischemic heart disease.  
5. **Endocrine Disruption:** Exposure to certain chemicals in indoor air has potential endocrine-disrupting effects, impacting metabolic health.  
6. **Neurological Effects:** Neurotoxic substances in household air pollutants can contribute to neurocognitive disorders and developmental delays.  
7. **Cancer:** Evidence suggests possible links between household air pollution and cancers, particularly due to exposure to toxins like benzene, VOCs, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).

## Strategies and Solutions to Mitigate Household Air Pollution  
Addressing household air pollution requires multi-faceted intervention strategies that minimize exposure and improve household air quality. Suggested approaches include:  

1. Promoting the use of clean and renewable energy sources such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), natural gas, or electricity for cooking and heating.  
2. Improving ventilation systems in homes, including the use of kitchen hoods and exhaust fans to minimize indoor smoke.  
3. Reducing the use of harmful chemicals in household cleaning products, furniture polish, and artificial fragrances.  
4. Encouraging the adoption of organic, non-toxic alternatives to pesticides and mosquito repellents.  
5. Educating households about the risks associated with tobacco smoke and encouraging smoke-free homes.  
6. Improving housing quality by choosing less toxic materials for construction, flooring, and furnishings.  
7. Developing low-cost, practical air purification solutions for low-income households.  

---

## Conclusion  
Household air pollution is an often-overlooked contributor to global morbidity and mortality. While progress has been made in understanding the adverse health effects of indoor pollutants, significant gaps remain in implementing strategies to reduce exposure. Public health initiatives and household-level interventions aimed at improving indoor air quality have the potential to drastically reduce the burden of diseases associated with household air pollution.

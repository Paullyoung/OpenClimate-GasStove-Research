**Environmental Health**  
**Near-highway pollutants in motor vehicle exhaust: A review of epidemiologic evidence of cardiac and pulmonary health risks**  
**Doug Brugge, John L Durant, Christine Rioux**  

**Abstract**  
There is growing evidence of a distinct set of freshly-emitted air pollutants downwind from major highways, motorways, and freeways, including elevated levels of ultrafine particulates (UFP), black carbon (BC), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), and carbon monoxide (CO). People living or spending substantial time within about 200 meters of highways are exposed to these pollutants more than persons living farther away, even compared to those living on busy urban streets. Evidence of the health hazards of these pollutants stems from studies assessing proximity to highways, actual exposure to the pollutants, or both. Collectively, health studies indicate elevated risks of asthma and reduced lung function in children living near major highways. Studies on particulate matter (PM) that link it to cardiac and pulmonary mortality also suggest increasing risk associated with smaller geographic areas of exposure, implying localized sources that likely include major highways. Although less research has been conducted on the association between lung cancer and highways, existing studies point to potential links as well. While the evidence substantiates the connection between near-highway exposures and adverse health outcomes, further research is needed to clarify the exact nature and scale of these risks.  

**Background**  
Approximately 11% of US households are located within 100 meters of 4-lane highways. Automobiles are known to be significant sources of air pollution, but the exposure of near-highway residents to pollutants in motor vehicle exhaust has only recently been characterized. Two primary reasons for this are:  
(A) Federal and state air monitoring programs typically measure pollutants at a regional, not local scale, and  
(B) Regional monitoring stations often do not measure all pollutant types that are elevated near highways.  

Thus, understanding near-highway exposures and their potential health consequences is critical.  

This review examines studies on the measurement of near-highway air pollutants and epidemiologic studies related to cardiac and pulmonary outcomes from exposure to these pollutants and/or living near highways. While some studies suggest additional health effects (e.g., birth outcomes), evidence for these is less developed and less likely to influence public policy. Literature from cellular biology and toxicology is only briefly discussed in this review due to its extensive scope.  

**Methods**  
The authors began their research by reviewing familiar studies while also conducting searches in engineering and health literature on Medline. Some earlier epidemiologic studies were identified through citations in more recent articles. Studies of motor-vehicle-related pollutants based on central site monitors—rather than highway proximity—are included when they enhance the plausibility of other findings. Emphasis was placed on the rigor of methodologies and the significance of findings. The review concludes with a summary and recommendations for policy and further research.  

**Motor Vehicle Pollution**  
Motor vehicle exhaust is a significant source of air pollution. Key pollutants include carbon monoxide, nitrogen and sulfur oxides, unburned hydrocarbons (from fuel and crankcase oil), particulate matter, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and other organic compounds derived from combustion. While much attention has been directed at the transformation and transport of these pollutants in ambient air—especially in areas with elevated human exposure (e.g., congested city centers, tunnels, and urban canyons created by tall buildings)—less focus has been placed on measuring pollutants and exposures near heavily-trafficked highways.  

Evidence from various studies indicates steep concentration gradients of certain pollutants near heavily traveled highways. These elevated pollution zones appear to have detrimental effects on human health.

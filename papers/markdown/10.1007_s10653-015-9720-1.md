**Air pollution and public health: emerging hazards and improved understanding of risk**

**Frank J. Kelly, Julia C. Fussell**  
**DOI:** 10.1007/s10653-015-9720-1  

---

**Abstract**  
Despite past improvements in air quality, large parts of the population in urban areas breathe air that does not meet European standards, let alone the health-based World Health Organisation Air Quality Guidelines. Over the past decade, there has been a significant increase in findings that particulate matter (PM) air pollution not only exerts greater impacts on established health endpoints but is also associated with a broader range of disease outcomes. Evidence suggests that effects have no threshold within the studied range of ambient concentrations, can occur at levels close to PM2.5 background concentrations, and follow a mostly linear concentration–response function.  

Having established this significant public health problem, considerable effort has gone into identifying the components of ambient PM that affect health and understanding the biological basis of its toxicity by identifying mechanistic pathways. This information aims to inform policymakers on how best to legislate for cleaner air. Additionally, achieving a healthier environment depends upon fostering public awareness and behavior change through optimal air pollution monitoring, forecasting, and reporting, supported by increasingly sophisticated information systems. Translating scientific evidence into bold, realistic, and effective policies has the potential to significantly reduce the burden of air pollution on public health.  

**Keywords:** PM toxicity, Emerging risks, Public awareness, Air quality communication

### **Introduction**  
#### **Historical Perspective**  
Air pollution is widely recognized as a significant public health problem, responsible for a growing variety of well-documented health effects. While urbanization exposes populations to unhealthy levels and a diverse mixture of ambient air pollutants, the issue has historical roots. Paleopathological research suggests that even ancient humans suffered from the consequences of smoke exposure. Computed tomography imaging studies on ancient mummies have detected signs of pneumonia, emphysema, pulmonary edema, and atherosclerosis (Zweifel et al., 2009; Thompson et al., 2013), and autopsies have revealed extensive carbon deposits in lungs (Zimmerman et al., 1971). These findings suggest a link to inhalation of smoke from fuels used for warmth, cooking, and lighting in confined spaces.  

Fast-forwarding to the Victorian era, the dense smoke and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) from domestic and industrial coal burning, combined with natural fog, became an infamous characteristic of London. Artists and writers like Charles Dickens and Claude Monet romanticized this "pea-souper" smog, depicting it as an emblem of metropolitan life. However, the deadly 1952 London smog marked a turning point, with lethal pollution trapped near ground level causing an estimated 4,000–12,000 deaths and severe respiratory and cardiovascular complications (Logan, 1953; Bell and Davis, 2001). This catastrophic event spurred the passage of the Clean Air Act in 1956, which curtailed domestic coal burning, created smokeless zones, and transitioned the UK to natural gas. Implementing these measures led to significant reductions in smoke and SO₂ concentrations (Wilkins, 1954; Fig. 1).

#### **Modern-Day Air Pollution**  
Despite historical progress, recent years have seen stalled improvements in urban air quality, both in the UK and worldwide. Modern pollution, unlike the visible Victorian smogs, is largely indiscernible but remains a critical issue. Poor air dispersal during certain conditions can lead to smog buildup, causing acute health episodes in susceptible individuals (e.g., those with asthma, COPD, or heart disease) who may require increased medications or hospitalization.

Of particular concern is the long-term exposure to modern pollution from particulate matter (PM) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), which has a cumulative impact on health. Trends in urbanization, industrial expansion, and increased vehicle usage have led to this pollutant mix, with emissions from diesel engines being especially problematic for producing fine PM and NO₂. Unlike earlier smog, which was visible and generated acute effects, today's pollutants silently contribute to chronic conditions, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases and even neurological effects.  

The current evidence underscores the need for stricter air quality standards and innovative control measures tailored to modern pollution challenges. Promoting public awareness and adopting clean energy technologies will be integral to mitigating these hazards. Further collaborative research is essential to refine our understanding of air pollution's health impacts and guide policies for a sustainable and health-conscious future.
